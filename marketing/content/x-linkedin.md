# X (Twitter) and LinkedIn

Secondary channels. X is for the build-in-public audience, which is not our ICP
but does produce useful feedback, early adopters, and occasional press. LinkedIn
reaches the more business-minded end of the coaching market, which skews toward
studio owners and hybrid coaches.

Neither will deliver your first 50 coaches. Both are cheap to run once the video
scripts and blog posts exist, because most of this is repurposed.

---

## X: the build-in-public thread

Post as a thread. First tweet has to stand alone.

> Fitness coaches lose up to 50% of their clients every year.
>
> I spent three months asking them why. It was almost never the programming.
>
> Here's what actually kills a coaching relationship: 🧵

> 1/ The average client relationship lasts 3 to 6 months.
>
> Not because the results stop. Because the attention does.

> 2/ Here's the number that broke my brain:
>
> ~60% of clients who ACHIEVED their goal still left.
>
> They won. And then they walked, because nothing came after the win.

> 3/ Every coach I asked told me a version of the same story:
>
> "They went quiet for a couple of weeks. I didn't really notice. Then the
> payment failed."
>
> Nobody quits over a bad workout. They quit over two weeks of silence.

> 4/ Which makes this a software problem.
>
> Every coaching platform I looked at opens on a client list. Alphabetical.
> By signup date. Whatever.
>
> The client crushing it and the client who hasn't logged in for 11 days get
> equal billing.

> 5/ So the coach does the triage in their head. Every morning. Forever.
>
> And the one thing a tired human is worst at noticing is an absence.
>
> Silence doesn't generate a notification.

> 6/ I'm building the inverse.
>
> The home screen isn't your client list. It's a ranked queue of who needs you
> today.
>
> At risk, first. Check-in due, second. On track, last, because they're fine.

> 7/ It's deliberately not for everyone.
>
> If you train alone, it's useless to you. It's for one person: the independent
> coach who actually gives a damn about every client.

> 8/ Private beta, free while we build.
>
> logbook.fit
>
> If you coach people for a living I'd genuinely like to hear how many days of
> silence would make you worry. I've got 7 as the default and I'm not sure.

## LinkedIn: long-form post

LinkedIn rewards a personal narrative with a business lesson. Front-load the
first two lines, everything after gets truncated behind "see more."

> A coach I know lost four clients in one month and couldn't tell me why.
>
> So we went through them one by one.
>
> None of them had complained. None had a bad experience they could point to.
> All four had simply gone quiet, somewhere between two and five weeks before
> they cancelled, and in every single case she found out when a payment failed.
>
> That's when it clicked for me: churn in coaching almost never announces itself.
> It's an absence, and absences don't send notifications.
>
> The industry numbers back it up. The average personal training relationship
> runs 3 to 6 months. Trainers lose up to half their client base annually. And
> the one that genuinely surprised me: research cited by the Personal Trainer
> Development Center found around 60% of clients who actually achieved their goal
> left anyway, because there was no follow-up plan.
>
> Sixty percent. Of the winners.
>
> Here's what I think is going on. Coaching software has spent a decade competing
> on the parts of the job that were already easy. Exercise libraries. Program
> builders. Video demos. All useful, all solved.
>
> Meanwhile the actual hard part, deciding who needs your attention today, is
> still done in the coach's head, at 6am, from memory, across a spreadsheet and
> nine WhatsApp threads.
>
> That's the part I'm building. Not another workout tracker. A workspace that
> opens on the three clients about to slip, and puts the ones who are fine at
> the bottom of the screen where they belong.
>
> It's called Logbook.fit. It's in private beta and it is deliberately useless to
> anyone who trains alone.
>
> If you coach people for a living: how long does a client have to go quiet
> before you'd want to know? I'd genuinely like to hear it, because my default is
> a guess.

## LinkedIn: shorter posts

> Unpopular opinion for the fitness industry:
>
> Your marketing problem is a retention problem wearing a disguise.
>
> If clients averaged 8 months instead of 4, you would need half the leads.
> Nobody wants to hear that, because fixing retention is unglamorous and building
> a funnel feels like progress.

---

> Three questions that predict whether a coaching business survives its third year:
>
> 1. Can you name, right now, which client hasn't contacted you in two weeks?
> 2. Do you have a plan ready for the client who's about to hit their goal?
> 3. When someone cancels, are you ever surprised?
>
> If the answer to #3 is "always", the other two are why.

---

## Cadence

- **X:** 1 thread every 2 weeks, 3 to 4 standalone posts a week. Reply to other
  people's posts more than you post. That's where the reach is.
- **LinkedIn:** 1 long-form a week, 2 short. Post Tuesday to Thursday mornings.
- **Repurpose ruthlessly.** Every video script in `short-form-video.md` is a
  standalone X post. Every blog post in `seo-articles.md` is a LinkedIn long-form.
  Write once, cut eight ways.
